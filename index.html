Where To Start First

Start with Problem Framing, then move to Personas, Pain Points, Metrics/North Star, UX Heuristics, User Journey and Frictions, PRD + AC + NFR, and finally Architecture and Integration. The Lifecycle Walkthrough below follows this exact sequence.

Rule of thumb: if a section cannot point to customer pain, evidence, and a measurable outcome, do not move to the next section yet.

Beginner Start-Here Path (One Pass)

Follow this exact sequence if you feel lost and want one connected story.

  1. Week 1: Learn discovery basics: prompting, Working Backwards, personas, pain framing, and evidence-based problem statements.
  2. Week 2: Convert pain into measurable direction: metrics pyramid, North Star, UX heuristic audits, and strategy synthesis.
  3. Week 3: Turn strategy into build-ready requirements: PRD sections, testable acceptance criteria, NFRs, dependencies, and risks.
  4. Week 4: Choose architecture using named constraints, threat-model it, diagram it, set SLOs, and negotiate trade-offs to ship a Technical Considerations Document (TCD).
  5. Week 5: Turn the TCD into a literal buildable system: a data model, a cloud topology, and a REST API contract (Technical Modeling Document, TMD).
  6. After each day: explain how that day's output becomes input for the next day. That is the connect-the-dots muscle.

Week 1 · Day 2

PR/FAQ (Working Backwards)

A one-page press release plus FAQ for a product that does not exist yet, written before any design or engineering work starts.

Why It Matters

It forces the team to prove customer value in plain language before a single engineer is staffed. If the press release is weak, the product idea is weak -- and that is cheap to discover on paper, expensive to discover in production.

What's Inside

  • Six-part PR: heading, sub-heading, summary, problem, solution, customer quote
  • The Five Customer Questions: who, what problem, one benefit, what evidence, what the moment of use looks like
  • Internal FAQ: riskiest assumption, build estimate, what breaks under partial adoption
Week 1 · Day 3

Validated Persona & JTBD

A persona built from observed behavior plus a Job-to-Be-Done statement, checked against a Validation Canvas instead of assumed from a title or age range.

Why It Matters

A persona built on demographics does not predict behavior. A validated 'who' and 'why' keeps every later prioritization call traceable to a real user, not an assumption the team fell in love with.

What's Inside

  • Persona Validation Canvas
  • Job-to-Be-Done statement (verb + circumstance, not a job title)
  • Behavioral evidence citations backing every trait
Week 1 · Day 5

Problem Statement + Evidence Brief

A six-line problem statement backed by an Evidence Brief that states data confidence and business impact.

Why It Matters

A problem statement without evidence is an opinion. This is the hard stop before metrics work starts -- leadership can challenge the problem before any resource is committed to solving it.

What's Inside

  • Six-line Problem Statement template
  • Evidence Brief with a stated confidence rating
  • Readout deck built for leadership challenge, not just approval
Week 2 · Days 1-2

Metrics Pyramid & North Star Metric

A three-tier metrics ladder (Operational Signals → KPIs → North Star) plus one North Star Metric, pressure-tested against three pitfalls and stress-tested with the Three Standard Challenges.

Why It Matters

Teams default to whatever metric is easiest to pull from a dashboard -- usually a vanity metric. The pyramid forces every signal to ladder into something a CEO and a front-line worker would both agree means the business is winning.

What's Inside

  • Metrics Tier Sheet (North Star / KPI / Operational Signals)
  • Believable Causal Chain -- a three-sentence story from signal to North Star
  • Three Standard Challenges: Causal Chain, Gameability, Instrumentation
  • North Star template with a counter-metric guardrail
Week 2 · Day 3

Heuristic Audit & Design Principles

A severity-scored UX audit against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics plus 3 TPM-specific lenses, distilled into 3 durable design principles.

Why It Matters

Subjective complaints like 'the form feels clunky' cannot be prioritized by engineering. Naming the exact heuristic and severity turns UX debt into a fundable ticket, and a design principle tells the team what to choose when two options compete.

What's Inside

  • Nielsen's 10 heuristics, scored pass / partial / fail
  • 3 TPM lenses: time-to-first-value, failure-mode dignity, power-user respect
  • 8-point Accessibility Floor checklist
  • 1-5 severity scoring guide
  • 3-Principle Card: one line + one decision it forces, per principle
Week 2 · Day 5

Customer Journey Map

A stage-by-stage map of the end-to-end customer journey that locates friction hotspots and evaluates each stage against the design principles.

Why It Matters

A heuristic audit finds isolated UX bugs. A journey map finds where those bugs compound across a real flow -- for example, a validation gap on one screen causing a support escalation three steps later. It turns isolated findings into one narrative of where the product currently fails the North Star.

What's Inside

  • Journey Map Canvas (stage, action, emotion, friction)
  • Friction hotspot list tied back to Week 1 pain points
  • Feature-prioritization frame (top 3 concepts, each tied to a friction point and a metric)
Week 3 · Days 1-5

Product Requirements Document (PRD)

An 11-section PRD skeleton -- drafted section by section across the week and peer-reviewed before it is called engineer-ready.

Why It Matters

The PRD is the first artifact engineering actually builds from. It is where five weeks of discovery, metrics, and design work either translate into a buildable document, or where a skipped step becomes visible as a section nobody can fill in.

What's Inside

  • §1-2 Context + Problem, grounded in Week 1 interviews and the Week 2 journey map
  • §3-4 Goals & non-goals + Scope (in / out)
  • §5 Solution sketch -- user-visible flow, no implementation detail
  • §6 Acceptance Criteria -- happy / sad / weird paths in Given/When/Then form
  • §7 NFRs across 5 categories: Performance, Reliability, Security, Usability, Maintainability
  • §8 Metrics & validation, §9 Risks & open questions, §10 Dependencies, §11 Out-of-scope follow-ups
  • Peer review scorecard before the draft is called engineer-ready
Week 4 · Day 1

Architecture Decision & Integration Map

A monolith-vs-microservices triage using named constraints pulled straight from the PRD (team size, deployment cadence, failure isolation needs), producing an integration map with explicit contracts between systems.

Why It Matters

The TPM's job here is not to make the architecture call for engineering -- it is to name the constraints that should drive it. Confusing those two roles is the most common way a TPM either overreaches or under-delivers at this stage.

What's Inside

  • Mono-vs-Micro Triage Pack
  • Named constraints sourced directly from the PRD
  • Integration / contract map engineering can actually scope against
Week 4 · Day 2

STRIDE Threat Model & Security NFRs (TCD §3)

A STRIDE threat-model walk across every integration boundary in the architecture, culled to the top 5 threats, translated into revised, architecture-level Security & Compliance NFRs that supersede the PRD's first-draft version.

Why It Matters

A TPM doesn't write the compliance program or design the mitigation -- but naming which compliance frame applies and pinning a threat to a specific arrow or box in the data flow is what turns a vague 'is this secure?' into a conversation a real security team can actually validate.

What's Inside

  • STRIDE walk across the architecture's data flow (boxes + arrows)
  • Top-5 threats, each with likelihood, impact, mitigation, and a named owner
  • Compliance frame check (SOC 2 / privacy regime / industry-specific)
  • Revised Security & Compliance NFRs (Requirement / Defense / Verification)
Week 4 · Day 3

C4 Context + Container Diagrams

Two C4-style diagrams -- Context (system + people + external neighbors) and Container (every independently deployable unit + how they talk) -- stress-tested against failure, trust-boundary, and evolvability lenses.

Why It Matters

A diagram a security reviewer or an outsider can follow in 90 seconds replaces architecture hand-waving with a shared visual vocabulary. The TPM's scope stops at Context + Container -- Component and Code levels are engineering's job.

What's Inside

  • C4 legend (owned-by-us vs. external, sync vs. async arrow, person)
  • Trust-boundary markup wherever data crosses from our control to someone else's
  • Three-Lens stress test: failure trace, trust boundary, evolvability
Week 4 · Day 4

Three SLOs + Latency-Budget Walk

A latency SLO, an availability SLO with an error budget, and a rate-limit policy, each passing a percentile + window + defense check, then walked hop-by-hop across the real Container diagram to verify the architecture can hit the number.

Why It Matters

An SLO without a percentile, a window, and a defense is an opinion wearing a number's clothes. The error budget converts an abstract percentage into a decision tool that tells a team objectively when to ship features versus when to stop and fix reliability.

What's Inside

  • Latency SLO: p95 ≤ 2.0s / p99 ≤ 5.0s, 30-day window
  • Availability SLO: 99.5%, ≈ 3.6 hr/month error budget
  • Rate-limit policy: per-user/per-tenant caps + 429 failure mode
  • Hop-by-hop latency-budget walk vs. the SLO target
Week 4 · Day 5

Top-5 Trade-Offs + TCD Shipped

Five technical trade-offs in strict Option A vs Option B → Choice → Accepted cost → Revisit trigger form, a stakeholder sign-off matrix with a named real person per constraint, and a full 6-section Technical Considerations Document (TCD) shipped after integration + cross-review.

Why It Matters

'We considered microservices but chose a monolith' is a description, not a trade-off -- naming the accepted cost and the revisit trigger is what separates senior architectural thinking from a feature list, and a constraint with no named owner never actually gets implemented.

What's Inside

  • 5 trade-offs spanning ≥ 3 tension categories
  • Stakeholder sign-off matrix (real names, honest status -- mostly 'Proposed,' not 'Approved')
  • 6-row integration checklist across all TCD sections
  • Cross-review + AI sanity check before shipping
Week 5 · Day 1

TMD Section 1 – Data Model

An entity model -- fields, keys, indexes tied to a numbered access pattern, cardinalities, invariants -- built only after every read and write the feature needs has been listed and numbered first.

Why It Matters

'Access Pattern First' exists because a schema designed before its queries are known is a schema that discovers slow queries in production; requiring every index to cite a numbered pattern keeps the model defensible to an engineer, not just plausible-looking.

What's Inside

  • Access Pattern Sheet (reads + writes, numbered, highest-volume reads circled)
  • Entity Model (one block per entity: fields/PK/indexes/relationships/invariants)
  • 3 storage trade-offs (normalization, consistency, schema flexibility)
  • AI schema critique with adopt/defer/reject log
Week 5 · Day 2

TMD Section 2 – Cloud Topology

Five topology decisions for the feature's real component map -- region + multi-AZ, managed-vs-self per component, multi-tenancy stance, network boundary, and a rough-order-of-magnitude monthly cost table -- closed with a required AI sanity check.

Why It Matters

Every topology choice trades cost against risk; defaulting to 'managed unless there's a specific reason not to' and 'pooled tenancy unless there's contractual evidence otherwise' stops a team from over-engineering for a problem no customer has actually asked for.

What's Inside

  • Region + multi-AZ stance with a named trade-off
  • Managed-vs-self table for every component (4-question test)
  • Multi-tenancy stance (pooled default) + network boundary (public HTTPS default)
  • ROM cost table with egress called out as its own line
Week 5 · Day 3

TMD Section 3 – REST API Contract

Every data-model entity checked as a resource candidate, URL paths and HTTP methods designed with correct happy/sad/weird-path status codes, and one endpoint documented end-to-end -- including idempotency, versioning, and the error-body shape.

Why It Matters

An API is a public contract that's hard to change later; the day trains catching two opposite mistakes at once -- a verb disguised as a resource (e.g. a '/reconcile' action endpoint) and blindly exposing every entity, including ones that are really internal plumbing a client should never touch directly.

What's Inside

  • Resource list, with exclusions reasoned (not every entity becomes an endpoint)
  • URL path design (nesting sub-resources one level deep)
  • Methods + status-code table for happy/sad/weird paths
  • One endpoint fully documented: idempotency key + window, versioning, error body

PR/FAQ (Working Backwards) — Filled Sample

Heading

FieldPulse Mobile Adds Offline Dispatch Assist, Cutting New-Dispatcher Escalations

Sub-heading

New dispatchers can now complete a full shift of job assignments without a senior dispatcher’s help, even with no signal.

Summary

FieldPulse Mobile ships an offline-ready dispatch assistant that validates job assignments in real time and queues changes for sync, so a new dispatcher’s first shift no longer depends on a senior teammate being available.

Problem

New dispatchers escalate to a senior teammate multiple times per shift because the app allows invalid job assignments to be saved silently, and offline signal drops erase unsaved work.

Solution

Inline validation blocks invalid saves before they happen, and an offline queue holds every change until connectivity returns, syncing automatically with no lost work.

“My first solo shift, I didn’t have to call anyone. The app told me exactly what was wrong before I hit save.” — sample customer quote

Internal FAQ

Riskiest assumption: dispatchers will trust an offline queue enough to keep working instead of waiting for signal.

Build estimate: 3 sprints for validation rules, 2 sprints for the offline queue and sync.

What breaks under partial adoption: if only some job types get validation rules, dispatchers will assume every field is validated — rollout has to cover every job type at once.

Validated Persona & JTBD — Filled Sample

Persona

Dana — New Dispatcher, first two weeks on the job. Hired for reliability, not software experience. Uses the mobile app one-handed while walking the floor.

Job to Be Done

“When I get a new list of jobs at the start of my shift, I want to assign each one correctly on the first try, so that I don’t have to interrupt a senior dispatcher to double-check my work.”

Behavioral evidence

  • Re-opened the same job assignment 4 times in one shift before asking for help (observed shadow session)
  • Waited an average of 6 minutes for a senior dispatcher to confirm a save before moving to the next job (support ticket logs)

Validation Canvas check

Confirmed against 5 shadow sessions and 12 support tickets — not assumed from job title or tenure alone.

Problem Statement + Evidence Brief — Filled Sample

Six-line Problem Statement

1. Who: new dispatchers in their first two weeks.

2. What: they cannot complete a full shift of job assignments without senior help.

3. Where/when: during job assignment, most often in the first 3 shifts.

4. Impact: senior dispatchers lose roughly 30 minutes per shift to escalations.

5. Root cause (hypothesis): no inline validation or offline recovery on the assignment form.

6. Opportunity: reduce escalations without adding a training program.

Evidence Brief

Confidence: high — corroborated by 12 support tickets and 5 shadow sessions.

Business impact: roughly 2.5 senior-dispatcher hours per week recovered for every 5 new hires onboarded.

Product Requirements Document (PRD) — Filled Sample

§1-2 Context + Problem (excerpt)

New dispatchers cannot complete a full shift independently because the assignment form allows invalid saves and loses work when offline.

§5 Solution Sketch (excerpt)

On Save, validate required fields inline; on connectivity loss, queue the change locally and sync automatically once signal returns.

§6 Acceptance Criteria (excerpt)

Given a dispatcher submits an assignment missing a required field, when they tap Save, then the form blocks the save and highlights the missing field.

Given a dispatcher is offline, when they save an assignment, then the change is queued and a “Pending Sync” badge is shown.

§7 NFR sample (Reliability)

Requirement: no queued change is lost across an app restart. Defense: the queue persists to local storage. Verification: kill-and-relaunch test with 10 queued changes.

This is a short excerpt — the full PRD runs all 11 sections end to end.

Architecture Decision & Integration Map — Filled Sample

Named Constraints (from the PRD)

  • Team size: 4 engineers
  • Deployment cadence: weekly
  • Failure isolation: offline sync must not block core dispatch

Triage Result

Modular monolith, with the offline-sync queue isolated behind its own internal service boundary and contract — full microservices would outrun a 4-engineer team’s deployment cadence.

Integration / Contract Map (excerpt)

Dispatch module → Sync Queue module: contract = {jobId, changeType, payload, clientTimestamp}, delivered at-least-once, deduplicated by clientTimestamp.

STRIDE Threat Model & Security NFRs (TCD §3) — Filled Sample

Top Threat (STRIDE walk excerpt)

Threat — Session token replay
STRIDE letter: Spoofing
Where: Backend service → Datastore arrow, right after session token issuance
Scenario: A captured token is replayed from a second device before it expires.
Likelihood: M   Impact: H
Mitigation: Bind the token to a device fingerprint with a 15-minute expiry — not "use HTTPS."
Owner: Security team (validate the binding approach)

Compliance Frame Check

  • SOC 2: user-action audit trail required (CC7.2), 24-month retention
  • Privacy: dispatcher PII processed — a state-level privacy regime applies
  • Industry-specific: none directly for this feature

Revised NFR (TCD §3)

Category: Security
Requirement: Session tokens are bound to a device fingerprint and expire after 15 minutes.
Defense: Directly mitigates the Spoofing threat found in the STRIDE walk.
Verification: Replay a captured token from a second device; access must be denied.

TCD §3 deepens the PRD’s §7 NFRs — it does not replace them.

Three SLOs + Latency-Budget Walk — Filled Sample

Latency-Budget Walk (sample)

Network-in 80ms → auth check 40ms → orchestration API 150ms (dominant hop) → database write 30ms → network-out 100ms = 400ms happy path, comfortably inside the SLO. But the API’s own 3-retry backoff policy pushes the true worst case to ≈4,200ms — still inside p99 ≤ 5.0s, but with only ≈800ms of headroom, not the ≈2,400ms the happy-path math alone implied.

Top-5 Trade-Offs + TCD Shipped — Filled Sample

Trade-Off (sample)

Tension: latency vs. durability. Option A: write the audit record synchronously. Option B: publish it async with a dead-letter queue. Choice: B. Accepted cost: a rare failure can lag the audit trail by up to 10 seconds. Revisit trigger: if audit-lag complaints appear in a compliance review.

Ship Status

TCD Status: Approved with gaps — honestly naming what is still open (an unresolved dependency, one trade-off flagged as under-defended) rather than rubber-stamping every row “Approved.”

TMD Section 1 – Data Model — Filled Sample

Access Pattern (sample)

Pattern #4 (★ highest-volume read): list open tickets for a shop, filtered by status and priority, ordered by priority then created-at — this single pattern drives the index design engineering needs on the Ticket table.

Storage Trade-Off (sample)

Strong consistency vs. replica reads: the open-ticket listing read must hit the primary database, not a replica, because a stale read could let two dispatchers claim the same ticket — directly protecting a concurrency invariant, not just a style preference.

TMD Section 2 – Cloud Topology — Filled Sample

Smell to Reject

“We want full control” is not a valid reason to self-manage a component -- the test asks whether failure modes match real needs, whether cost is reasonable at this scale, whether compliance allows it, and whether there is a SPECIFIC reason to control it.

Cost Surprise (sample)

The highest-availability database tier can consume well over a third of the entire monthly infrastructure budget — the direct dollar cost of an availability SLO, made visible instead of buried in an abstract percentage.

TMD Section 3 – REST API Contract — Filled Sample

Deliberate Exclusion (sample)

An internal outbox table used only to relay events to a background process is deliberately NOT exposed as a REST resource — exposing it would let a client bypass the exact audit or billing guarantee it exists to provide.

Idempotency (sample)

An Idempotency-Key header with a 24-hour window turns a “network dropped mid-submit, client retries” scenario into a safe replay instead of a duplicate side effect.
